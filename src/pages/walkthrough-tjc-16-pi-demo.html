How to demo the rendezvous plots to your PI: Case B (quarter period)

This is a script for a 10 to 15 minute demo. It follows the order in which the pipeline produces its plots, so the story builds as the plots appear. Every number below came from one real run of Case B on this project's code with real GMAT: outcome VERIFIED, comparison PASS.

1. The problem in one minute

SayA target spacecraft is in a circular orbit 400 km up. A chaser sits 1000 m ahead of it, along the orbit, and is not moving relative to it. We want the chaser to meet the target exactly, with zero relative speed, in a quarter of an orbit, using two short engine burns.
ItemValue
OrbitCircular, 400 km altitude, orbit radius 6778.137 km, Earth mu = 398600.4418 km³/s²
Orbital period5553.62 s, so a quarter is 1388.406 s (about 23 minutes)
Chaser start1000 m ahead of the target, zero relative velocity
GoalRelative position 0, relative velocity 0, at 1388.406 s
BurnsTwo impulsive (instant) burns: one at departure, one at arrival

Read the axes once, and then reuse them

All plots use the target's moving frame (called LVLH). The target is always at the origin.

  • x is radial: positive means higher, away from Earth.
  • y is along-track: positive means ahead of the target on the orbit.
  • z is cross-track: sideways from the orbit plane. It stays 0 in this case.

2. Start the run

Type the request into the project's agent session. The standing rule routes it through formalizer, model selector, workflow and GMAT, in that order, even without typing the slash command.

/rendezvous Earth is the central body, R_earth = 6378.137 km, μ = 398600.4418 km³/s². The target is in a circular orbit at 400 km altitude. LVLH frame: x radial outward, y along-track, z cross-track, right-handed. At t = 0, the chaser relative position is [0 m, 1000 m, 0 m] and relative velocity is [0 m/s, 0 m/s, 0 m/s]. Use two impulsive burns, one at departure and one at arrival. After 1388.406068 seconds (one quarter of the target's orbital period), reach relative position [0 m, 0 m, 0 m] and relative velocity [0 m/s, 0 m/s, 0 m/s]. Return the two burn vectors and terminal relative state.
Before the meeting: do a dry run so GMAT's start-up time is not a surprise. In the run I checked, the plots appeared as three separate artifacts in this order: 3D scene, trajectory plot, comparison plots. If a plot does not appear, say so and do not substitute another figure; the pipeline is built to report "not generated" rather than fake one.

4. Plot 2: trajectory and burns

SayThis is the plan. The chaser starts 1000 m ahead (top of the plot, burn 1). A burn pushes it up and backward, it drifts along a curved arc, and it arrives at the target (the triangle at the origin) just as burn 2 stops it.
Marker in legendWhat it means here
Chaser path (blue line)Predicted relative position over the 1388 s. The arc bulges to about 252 m above the target's orbit. I computed this from the solution's sample points.
Target (triangle)The origin. By definition the target never moves in this frame.
Burn 1 (circle)At the start, (0, 1000) m. Velocity change [0.688, -0.344, 0] m/s: up and backward.
Burn 2 (square)At the end. Velocity change [0.688, +0.344, 0] m/s. It cancels the arrival velocity [-0.688, -0.344, 0] m/s exactly, leaving zero relative speed.
Rendezvous (star)Target state: position 0 and velocity 0.

The total cost is 1.539 m/s of delta-v. The path is a curve, not a straight line, because the chaser and target are both in orbit; being slightly higher or lower changes how fast you move along the orbit. That is the physics the Clohessy-Wiltshire (CW) model captures: a linearized model of relative motion near a circular orbit.

Likely PI question: "Why not just thrust straight at it?" Answer: in orbit, thrust changes your orbit, not just your position, so the useful path is a curve that the CW equations compute for you.

5. Plot 3: CW prediction against GMAT

SayThe plan came from a simplified model, so we do not take its word for it. GMAT is an independent NASA-developed mission tool. We fed it the same two burns and recorded where it says the chaser goes. If the two lines lie on top of each other, the simple model was good enough for this case.
  • The blue solid line is the CW prediction. The orange dashed line is GMAT's.
  • The lines should overlap to the eye. The difference is shown on the next plot.
  • The z axis is essentially flat in this case, because there is no sideways motion.

6. Plot 4: how far apart are they?

SayThis answers "how close is close?". The red line is the gap between the two models. The dotted line is the limit I set for calling it a match. Red below dotted means PASS.
QuantityLargest gapLimit
Position error0.196 m, at the final sample (t = 1388 s)1 m
Velocity error0.000298 m/s, at the final sample0.001 m/s
  • The error is largest at the end, so you can say: the models drift apart slowly over the 23 minutes, never exceeding 0.2 m on a 1000 m approach.
  • GMAT's own final state after both burns is about [0.147, -0.130, 0] m and [0.00013, -0.00027, 0] m/s. So the chaser ends about 20 cm from the target in GMAT, moving at about 0.3 mm/s.
  • Likely cause of the gap: CW assumes the offset is small relative to the orbit radius, while GMAT uses full gravity. That is my reading, not something I measured separately.
Likely PI question: "Is PASS a proof?" No. It means this case matched GMAT within the stated limits, 1 m and 1 mm/s. Those limits are a project choice, so be ready to say why they were chosen.

7. Close with these three sentences

  1. The solver planned two burns totalling 1.54 m/s for a 1 km, quarter-orbit rendezvous.
  2. An independent tool, GMAT, flew the same burns and ended within 0.2 m and 0.3 mm/s of the target.
  3. The plots appear at each stage so a human can check the story at each step, not just trust a final PASS.

What this demo does not show

  • It is one case, run once. It is a small offset near a circular orbit, where CW is expected to work. It says nothing about large separations or elliptical orbits.
  • The 3D target placement is illustrative.
  • The plots here are from my run of the project code in a separate worktree at the live-display commit, not from the final range head.
  • I wrote the plot explanations from the plotting code's labels and the computed numbers. Look at each image once in your dry run before you present.
